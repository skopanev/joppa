One workspace. One product.

A product can span several repositories. They share one requirement tree. Tasks link to repositories: one acceptance criterion may need work in the app, backend and infrastructure.

Switching workspace changes the entire tree, requirements and discussions.

The product tree and its tasks

Domain → Capability → REQ → AC describes what the product must do. Tasks record the work needed to deliver it.

  1. Domain

    Question it answers
    Which part of the business is this?
    Example
    Subscriptions and access.

    1. Capability

      Question it answers
      What can the product do?
      Example
      Restore paid access.

      1. REQ Requirement

        Question it answers
        What must the product guarantee?
        Example
        A customer with an active subscription keeps paid access after signing in again.

        1. AC Acceptance criterion

          Question it answers
          What must be true to accept this requirement?
          Example
          On iOS, a customer with an active subscription has access to paid features after signing in again.

          1. Task Work linked to ACs

            Question it answers
            What work needs to be done?
            Example
            Implement paid-access restoration after sign-in in the iOS app.

            Each task belongs to a REQ revision, has an assignee and links to one or more ACs. One AC may need several tasks.

Open a requirement to see its Tasks section. Tasks can be technical or operational, such as restricting a key’s permissions before launch. Completing a task records work done; the linked AC still needs a passed check.

Domains and capabilities organize commitments. Verification and acceptance apply to requirements and their ACs.

Status circles show green for passed AC checks or accepted requirements, yellow for pending decisions or ongoing work, red for failed checks, and grey for unchecked or empty items. Capability and domain circles summarize all their current children, including those hidden by filters: any failure is red, all complete is green, all unchecked or empty is grey, and mixed progress is yellow. An empty branch cannot be green. Task completion does not make a requirement green. Historical revisions use grey circles; these indicators do not establish verification of the currently deployed release.

Open a domain, capability, requirement or AC and choose the copy + robot button. Paste the unique system ID and title into your agent chat to discuss it. Content changes, discussions, decisions and acceptance are recorded through Joppa MCP. The portal is read-only, including comments, descriptions, attachments and tree order. Use the dropdowns to choose what to view. The history icon opens a compact list of when, who and what changed. A requirement’s revision number sits beside it; choose an older revision inside History to read its wording and evidence.

File and comment icons are highlighted with a count when content exists. Empty icons stay thin, muted and disabled. Choose an active file icon beside a domain, capability, requirement, AC, intake or intake point to open the file list and read or download a file; adding or removing files goes through your agent. The notes button opens read-only descriptions and discussion. The copy button gives your agent the system ID and title on one line.

Who shapes the tree?

An agent proposes domains and capabilities from the product and its documentation. The product owner reviews boundaries and decision owners, then confirms the structure.

People and agents write requirements and ACs together. Each REQ has one owner who responds to suggestions and accepts the result. Legal, compliance and leadership record decisions on the same requirement through MCP.

The agent assesses each AC. Missing information becomes a specific question addressed to someone. Independent, ready ACs can proceed while others await a decision.

What a check proves

A passed check supports one acceptance condition for one REQ revision on a recorded combination of builds and configuration. A completed task alone proves none of that.

Before execution, the runner records the full expected check list and whether discovery is complete. Every expected check needs a result. Missing, skipped or undiscovered checks cannot make an AC green. One automated test names one AC; several tests may check the same AC.

Make a result reproducible
RecordExample
Requirement and conditionPaid access after sign-in, revision 1, iOS access AC.
Versions and environmentiOS build A1 + backend B1 + configuration C1, tested in staging. Use immutable build IDs or commit hashes.
Input and stepsA fixture with an active subscription; sign out, then sign in. Identify the fixture version in the run or its evidence.
Expected and observedExpected: paid features open. Observed: the purchase screen appears.
EvidenceThe recorded result and inspection note, with a log, report or screenshot link when available. An attachment or link alone does not establish a pass.

For a product spanning repositories, “version” means the relevant combination, not whichever repository changed last. A name such as “staging” identifies a place; it does not identify its build or configuration. Include the versions that can affect the condition.

A manual check needs concrete evidence too.

For a key that must have exactly read, verify, record that expected list and the actual list. An extra delete permission means failure. “Checked manually” is insufficient. Only a human submits manual results; an agent records a human decision only on explicit instruction.

Read green as “verified on these versions”. The portal currently summarizes the latest-started completed run. It does not automatically compare that run with the deployed release. Inspect the versions in Checks before treating a green card as evidence about production. While a newer run is still running, the previous completed result remains visible.

One requirement, from work to a new release

Illustrative build labels below stand for immutable versions. The agreed scope is iOS; Android and web require their own scope decision.

  1. Agree on the behavior

    REQ: A customer with an active subscription keeps paid access after signing in again. AC: On iOS, paid features are available after sign-in for that customer.

    The owner confirms revision 1. The agent assesses its AC. Two tasks deliver it: the backend returns the subscription entitlement, and the iOS app refreshes access after sign-in.

  2. Record the plan, then run it

    The runner seals the full check list for app A1 + backend B1 + configuration C1. It records the test environment and fixture. Both tasks can be complete while the AC still awaits verification.

  3. Keep the failure and fix the cause

    Failed The backend reports an active subscription, but the app shows the purchase screen. The observed behavior and report stay in history.

    The agent links the correction task to the AC. After the app fix, the runner records a fresh plan and reruns the full expected set on app A2 + backend B1 + configuration C1. The requirement wording is unchanged, so it remains revision 1.

  4. Verify, then ask the owner to accept

    Passed Every expected check passes on that same combination. The REQ is ready for acceptance. The owner explicitly accepts revision 1 and its checked run. Agent confidence, task completion and silence cannot substitute for this decision.

  5. Check the next release on its own versions

    Backend B2 is released later. The successful A2/B1/C1 report remains a valid historical record; it does not verify A2/B2/C1. Run the checks for the new combination, even when the REQ wording is unchanged, then obtain acceptance of the new successful run.

    Before launch, compare the intended release with the accepted evidence. Joppa’s launch check can reject a mismatched version for mandatory conditions, but it blocks a deployment only when the release pipeline actually enforces it.

Changed wording needs fresh evidence

Saving a change to REQ or AC wording creates a new REQ revision. All ACs in that revision start at n/a, including unchanged ones. Earlier checks and acceptance remain attached to their original revision. Even a typo correction in that wording currently requires fresh results and acceptance.

The agent compares the implementation and tests with the new wording, updates them where needed and reruns the full checks. Suitable test code can be reused; old results cannot be copied into the new revision.

Comments do not create revisions or automatically block work. An agent must not silently expand an iOS condition to Android or web. People decide changes to scope and policy.

When work needs another pass

What happens next, and who moves it forward
SituationNext action
A check failsThe agent investigates and links the fix to the AC, then reports a fresh complete run. A newer completed failure cannot be hidden by choosing an older pass.
Results are missing or skippedThe runner completes discovery and execution in a fresh run. The AC stays unverified until the full expected set passes.
A deployed build differsThe agent checks the new combination. Old evidence stays in history and is not proof about the new build.
The owner finds a defect or declines acceptanceThe owner records the reason. A confirmed defect invalidates the affected result and removes acceptance. After resolution, a fresh passing run is needed. Changed expected behavior requires a new revision.
Requirements or policy conflictThe agent records the specific question and responsible decision maker, marking the affected AC as waiting. Its dependents wait; independent ready ACs may proceed.

History preserves wording, reports and decisions with their authors and timestamps. Acceptance records what the owner considered sufficient for that revision and run; it does not prove that every possible condition has been discovered.
